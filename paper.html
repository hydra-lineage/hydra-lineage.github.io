Forward motion in a decentralised swarm

How code changes travel between independent, AI-assisted projects working on the same problem: how much, by what route, through whom, and whether the projects are pooling their work or drifting apart.

Figures as of 28 November 2026

Abstract. When many independent teams attack one problem in public, with AI coding agents in visible use and no coordinating body, does the group advance as a whole or does each team advance alone? We study 236 projects under 231 separate owners, all building compatibility software for a single console title, over the 8.6 days between the creation of the first repository and this snapshot. They are those of the 281 repositories on record that originated at least 2 changes of their own. From the commit graph we trace every change from the project that first held it to every project that later carried it, and we separate what a project was founded on, what it mirrored, and what it deliberately merged or re-applied.

Across 75,527 commits we identify 47,336 distinct changes. 9,035 of them (19.1%) were taken up by a project of another owner, and half of those were first taken up within 8.0 hours of appearing. Of 20,835 take-ups, 70.5% came down the taking project's own upstream line, 12.5% flowed back up a line, and 17.0% moved sideways between projects that are not on each other's line; 5.5% of all changes moved sideways at least once. Depending on how a take-up is defined, the share of changes that travel lies between 1.2% and 29.6%. Half of everything taken up comes from 6 projects, and with the five most involved projects removed 36.0% of take-ups remain, against 96.6% when five are removed at random. Changes carrying an AI agent signature (29.9% of all changes) were taken up more often than changes without one, and the difference within originating projects is narrower than the raw difference.

Whether the projects are pooling their work is measured from what each holds over time. 49.0% of changes are held under a single owner, and the median overlap between the holdings of two projects in one family has fallen from 98.7% to 19.7%. Over the window, the share of a day's new changes taken up within six hours is higher on the last such day than on the first, without rising every day. Of the pull requests on record that were decided, 77.7% of those from other projects in the study were merged and 63.8% of those from outside it. A rights holder filed 5 takedown notices over the period, 1 of them posted during the study; around the one that can be assessed, the number of repositories created does not depart from what other hours of the study show. Self-reported progress, where a public tracker records it, cannot be tied to take-up on this record. At this snapshot, exchange in the population runs mainly along lines of foundation.

1. Introduction

Open-source development has long been described as production without central direction: contributors choose their own work, and what they produce becomes a shared resource that others build on [1, 2]. In practice that sharing has usually had a centre. Under the pull-based model that dominates public code hosting, contributors work in forks and a small group of maintainers decides what enters one agreed repository [3]. The centre is where progress accumulates.

Two developments weaken the assumption that there will be a centre. Forking a repository costs nothing and takes seconds, so a project can be copied many times before any shared process has formed. And AI coding agents lower the cost of producing changes, so each copy can move quickly without help from the others. The result can be a population of repositories with a common starting point, a common goal, and no agreed place where work is integrated. Whether such a population makes cumulative progress is an open empirical question. One team may make an improvement, a second may take it and build on it, and a third may take the result from the second. If that happens routinely, the population advances together although nobody is in charge. If it does not, the population is a set of parallel efforts that share an origin.

Such a population is also exposed. A repository can be withdrawn by its owner or disabled by the platform, and a notice from a rights holder can reach every fork in a network at once. What the remaining projects hold in common, and what is created afterwards, bear on the same question.

This paper measures what is happening in one dense and public case. We do not ask whether the software works or which project is ahead, and we take no position on the projects, on what they build, or on the notices filed against repositories in their field. The case is of interest only as an instance of decentralised development that can be observed from public records. The research questions are:

  • RQ1: structure and roles. How are the projects related by shared history, and what part does each play in the exchange?
  • RQ2: extent and route. What share of changes is taken up by a project of another owner, and by what route relative to that structure?
  • RQ3: concentration and dependence. Who supplies the work that is taken up, who takes it, who carries it, and how much of the exchange rests on a few projects?
  • RQ4: speed and change over time. How long after a change appears is it taken up, and how did take-up, delay and route change over the study's life?
  • RQ5: what travels. Is take-up associated with an AI agent signature, with the size or kind of a change, or with the part of the code it touches, and does signed work show a different working rhythm?
  • RQ6: pooling or fragmenting. Are the projects' holdings growing more alike or drifting apart, how much work is repeated, and do projects keep working?
  • RQ7: offered and accepted. What happens to pull requests, and how much take-up arrives through them?
  • RQ8: outcomes. Is take-up associated with self-reported progress?
  • RQ9: takedown notices. How does the creation of repositories, and the exchange between them, stand before and after a notice?
  • RQ10: robustness. How far do the answers depend on the definitions?

The paper makes five contributions. First, a method for tracing changes across a population of related and unrelated repositories using only the commit graph, timestamps and patch fingerprints, with definitions that separate inherited history, mirrored history and deliberate take-up. Second, a classification of each take-up by its route relative to the taking project's own line of foundation, and of each project by its role in the exchange. Third, measures of whether a population without a centre is converging: the overlap between projects' holdings over time, the dependence of exchange on its most involved projects, and the rate at which identical work is repeated. Fourth, a complete measurement of one population, kept in a permanent ledger so that projects which later disappear remain in the record, and set beside the platform's public record of takedown notices. Fifth, a sensitivity analysis showing how much the headline figure moves under each defensible alternative definition. Every figure in the paper is recomputed from the ledger whenever the study runs.

2. Background and related work

2.1 Forks and families of projects

A fork in the older sense is a split: a copy of a project that goes its own way under different maintainers. Robles and González-Barahona catalogued significant forks of this kind with their causes and outcomes [6]. On current hosting platforms the word also covers the routine copy a contributor makes in order to propose changes. Jiang and colleagues study why developers fork and report that they do so mainly to submit pull requests, fix bugs and add features [9]. Zhou, Vasilescu and Kästner revisit hard forks on a modern platform and report that they often grow out of ordinary contribution forks and are perceived less negatively than the earlier literature suggests [7]. The same authors show that communities differ widely in how much work is lost or duplicated across forks, and that modular design and centralised management are associated with more contributions and a higher share of accepted pull requests [8]. Kalliamvakou and colleagues warn that a repository is not necessarily a project: the forks of one repository are often best understood as parts of a single effort [4]. Our population contains both kinds of fork and also repositories that were pushed as fresh copies with no declared parent, so we do not rely on the platform's fork relation. We reconstruct relatedness from shared commits, as Mockus and colleagues do at the scale of all public repositories [15].

2.2 Propagation of changes between variants

The closest prior work studies how patches move between long-lived variants of a system. Ray and Kim examine the BSD family and find that porting patches from sibling projects is a substantial and persistent part of maintaining a fork [11]. Stănciulescu, Schulze and Wąsowski follow forks of an open-source firmware and how features migrate from forks back to the main line [10]. Businge and colleagues study families of variant forks within three software ecosystems and how they are maintained alongside their mainlines [12]. Ramkisoen and colleagues detect patches that were applied in one variant and are missing or independently repeated in another, and report that missed patches had on average existed in the source variant for about a year [13]. German, Adams and Hassan track individual commits through the many repositories of the Linux kernel community and show that the path a commit takes can be recovered by observing repositories continuously [14]. At the level of content, Lopes and colleagues show that a large part of the code on a major hosting platform is duplicated between repositories [16].

This study differs from that work in three respects. The time scale is hours and days, not years. The population has no agreed mainline, so direction of flow is a result and not an assumption. And we separate the routes by which a change can come to be present in a second project, because presence alone conflates inheritance, mirroring and choice.

2.3 What Git history can and cannot say

Bird and colleagues set out the hazards of mining Git: history can be rewritten, a commit does not record the branch or repository it was made in, and identical work can appear under several commit identifiers after rebasing or cherry-picking [5]. Flint, Chauhan and Dyer document the pitfalls of time-based Git data, incorrect timestamps among them, and how they affect analyses [17]. Both hazards bear directly on this study, which must decide where a change originated and when it arrived somewhere else. Section 4 states the rules we use and Section 7 states where they fail.

2.4 AI coding agents in open source

Evidence on AI assistance has so far concerned the individual developer or the individual project. A controlled experiment found that developers with an AI pair programmer completed a set task faster [18], while a randomised trial with experienced open-source maintainers working on their own repositories found that allowing AI tools made them slower [19]. At repository scale, Li, Zhang and Hassan assemble a large dataset of pull requests authored by autonomous agents [20], Watanabe and colleagues find that most agent-authored pull requests in their sample were accepted, a substantial part of them after further human changes [21], and He and colleagues associate adoption of an AI editor with a transient rise in development velocity and a persistent rise in code complexity [22]. Dey and colleagues identify automated committers from the regularity of their activity and messages as well as from their names [30]. None of these asks what happens between projects. If agents make changes cheap to produce, the binding constraint on collective progress may move from producing changes to noticing and integrating those made by others.

2.5 Concentration, roles and acceptance

That a small core does most of the work is among the oldest findings about open-source projects [27], and Crowston and Howison show that projects differ widely in how centralised their communication is [28]. Networks whose connections are concentrated in a few nodes keep working when nodes fail at random and break up when the best-connected nodes are removed [29]. We apply that test to the exchange of changes. On what is accepted, Gousios and colleagues describe how pull requests are handled [3] and Tsay, Dabbish and Herbsleb find that the submitter's connection to a project bears on acceptance alongside the content of the contribution [32]. Hindle, German and Holt classify commits by purpose from their messages [31], and Avelino and colleagues study how often projects are abandoned by the developers who carry them and what follows [33].

2.6 Removal and what follows it

Zhang and Li analyse the takedown notices that the hosting platform studied here publishes, as a record of dispute resolution [34]. Evidence on what removal does comes mainly from other fields. Aguiar, Claussen and Peukert find that shutting down a dominant unlicensed streaming site reduced unlicensed consumption only briefly and left a more fragmented set of successors [35], while Danaher and Smith find that the shutdown of a large file-hosting service was followed by higher licensed sales [36]. Hagenbach and Koessler model the conditions under which an attempt to suppress information draws attention to it [37]. In software, Gamalielsson and Lundell follow a community that continued under a new name after a fork forced by a change of stewardship [38]. None of these observes the code of the affected projects hour by hour around a removal, which the ledger used here permits within the limits set out in Section 6.4.

3. Study setting and data

3.1 The case

The case is a set of independent teams building compatibility software for a single console title. The first repository in the population was created on 20 November 2026 and this snapshot was taken on 28 November 2026, an observation window of 8.6 days. The median project is 3 days old, and 90 of the 236 projects (38.1%) were created in the 72 hours before the snapshot. Every figure below describes the population at that age.

3.2 Assembling the population

The starting point is a public community list of repositories working on the problem, which named 83 repositories at this snapshot; 13 of them could not be reached. For each listed repository we queried the hosting platform for its hundred most recently pushed forks, kept those that had been pushed to after they were created, and admitted up to 25 per listed repository. Any repository named as the fork parent of an admitted repository was admitted as well. Repositories larger than 600 megabytes are skipped, and the record is capped at 260 repositories: listed repositories and those already recorded come first, then forks in order of most recent push.

281 repositories have recorded history. A recorded repository is a project in the study only if it originated at least 2 changes of its own, with origins attributed over all recorded repositories as Section 4.1 defines them. 45 repositories (16.0%) fall below that bar: 34 originated no change and 11 originated fewer than 2. 37 of them only mirror another repository and 3 took a change up. They remain in the ledger. The 11 changes they originated are left out of every count and are not credited to any project, and a commit made in an excluded repository is treated as made outside the study.

The study population is the remaining 236 projects under 231 owners: 78 from the list or named as parents, and 158 found through fork listings. An owner is the account a repository sits under. Every branch of every repository is read, not only the default branch, and no history is truncated; 195 projects have more than one branch and the population has 11,355 branch heads in all. Once a repository has been recorded it stays in the ledger. If it later ceases to be public, its commit graph as last seen is kept and it is marked as gone from the run that first failed to find it. The number of projects in that state at this snapshot is 20.

3.3 What is recorded from Git

For every commit the study keeps its identifier, its parents, the author time and commit time, a keyed hash standing in for the author, the family of AI agent whose signature the commit carries, if any, the number of files and lines changed, a fingerprint of the patch, and the subject line with anything that could identify a person removed. No source code is kept. The patch fingerprint is Git's stable patch identifier, which is the same for two commits that make the same textual change regardless of where in history they sit.

The ledger holds 75,527 commits. 20,089 are merges and 55,438 are not; 351 of the latter yield no fingerprint, typically because they change no file content, and are left out. 38,497 commits (51.0%) are reachable from more than one project. The method in Section 4 is a set of rules for deciding what each instance of that overlap means. The changes carry 2,273 distinct author identities.

3.4 Supplementary records

Four further records are kept, each from a public source.

  • Areas. For each non-merge commit, the first two directory levels of every file it changes, with the number of files in each. File names are not kept.
  • Pull requests. For each project, the pull requests the platform reports: when each was opened, whether it is open, merged or closed, when it was decided, whether it came from another repository and, if so, whether that repository is in the study, a keyed hash of its author, its size, and the commit that merged it. The record holds the most recent few hundred for each project.
  • Self-reported progress. A public community tracker reads each listed project's own statement of how far it has got and places it on a ladder of stages. The study keeps each reading with its time, and for projects at the upper stages the finer performance readings the tracker also records. These are the projects' own claims as read by a third party.
  • Takedown notices. The hosting platform publishes every takedown notice it processes. For each notice filed by the rights holder concerned, the study keeps where the notice is published, the time the platform posted it, the number of repositories it names, any count of forks or of a whole network that it states, and which recorded repositories it names. A named repository is matched to the record by a keyed hash of its owner together with its repository name; names and addresses of named repositories are not kept.

4. Method

4.1 Definitions

Write κ(p) for the time project p was created on the platform, and τ(c) for the commit time of commit c. A project holds every commit reachable from any of its branches. Each definition below is followed by the reason for it and by what it gets wrong.

Change
An equivalence class of non-merge commits that share a patch fingerprint and an author identity. Cherry-picks, rebases and single-commit squash merges give a piece of work a new commit identifier but leave both of these unchanged, so they collapse into one change. Why: the commit identifier follows history, not work, and the same work must be recognised in a second project. What it misses: work that was re-typed, adapted to resolve a conflict, or squashed from several commits has a new fingerprint and counts as a new change, so borrowing is undercounted. Requiring the same author keeps two people who arrive at the same patch separately from being merged into one change; 348 fingerprints occur in changes that originate under more than one owner and are kept apart for that reason.
Arrival
The arrival α(p, c) of a commit in a project that holds it is τ(c) if c lies on the first-parent line of one of the project's branches, and otherwise τ(m) for the earliest merge m on such a line that brings c in; m is then the commit that carried it. Why: a merge commit is the only record Git keeps of when existing history joined a branch. What it misses: a fast-forward leaves no record, so history that arrives that way keeps its original times and appears to have arrived when it was written. Branches are read default branch first, and a commit is dated by the first branch on which it is found.
Origin
The origin of a commit is the recorded repository where it arrived first. Ties, which arise whenever repositories share history, go to one where the commit is on a first-parent line, then to one in which the commit's author has other work found nowhere else, then to the older repository. A commit that first entered a repository inside a merge is assigned the origin of the merge that carried it. The origin of a change is the origin of its earliest-authored commit, and t0 is that commit's arrival there. Origins are attributed once, over every recorded repository, before the inclusion rule of Section 3.2 is applied, and are not revised afterwards. Why: Git does not record where a commit was made, so the earliest holder is the best available evidence. What it misses: work made in a repository outside the record is credited to the first recorded repository that merged it, and where a contribution was fast-forwarded into an older repository the older repository can be credited.
Founding
A commit with α(p, c) earlier than κ(p) belongs to the founding inheritance of p: the project already carried it when its repository was created. Founding shows what a project was built on and is never counted as take-up. What it misses: older history that reaches a project later by fast-forward keeps its original times and is counted as founding.
Take-up
A change is taken up by project p when p is not its origin, p has a different owner from its origin, the change arrives at or after κ(p), and the commit that carried it was made in p. A take-up is merged if the carrier is a merge commit made in p, and re-applied if p made its own commit with the same fingerprint and author, as a cherry-pick, a rebase or a single-commit squash does. Why: in both cases someone acting in p performed an operation whose effect was to bring the change in. What it misses: a merge brings in everything on the merged branch, so a merged take-up shows that the project accepted a body of history, not that it selected each change in it.
Mirroring
A change that arrives in p after κ(p) carried by a commit made elsewhere, as when a fork is fast-forwarded to the project it follows. Mirrored arrivals are counted separately and are not take-ups: they show that a project tracks another, and they carry no usable arrival time.
Reach and lag
The reach of a change is the number of distinct owners, other than the origin's, that took it up. The lag of a take-up is its arrival time minus t0, set to zero in the 377 cases (1.8%) where recorded times place the take-up before the origin.

4.2 The founding tree

Each project is assigned at most one foundation: among the projects created before it, the one that already held the largest part of its founding inheritance at the moment it was created. Ties go to the candidate that originated more of that inheritance, then to the parent declared on the platform, then to the oldest. The assignment is made only if the candidate held at least three commits and at least a fifth of the inheritance. These links form a forest. A project with no foundation is a root, a root together with everything founded on it directly or indirectly is a family, and the depth of a project is the number of links between it and its root. The upstream line of a project is every project that can be reached from it by following foundation links and declared fork-parent links in any combination.

4.3 Route of a take-up

Counting take-ups answers whether work moves. It does not answer whether projects choose work from each other, because a fork that merges from the project it was forked from produces take-ups by doing nothing more than keeping up. We therefore assign each take-up one of five routes, tested in this order:

  • Down the line, direct. The origin is on the taking project's upstream line.
  • Down the line, relayed. The origin is not on the line, but a project on the line already held the change when the taker received it. The change was available from the taker's own upstream, which had it from somewhere else.
  • Back up the line. The taking project is on the origin's upstream line: work made in a descendant was taken by a project it is founded on. This is the ordinary direction of a contribution.
  • Sideways, same family. None of the above, and both projects have the same root.
  • Sideways, across families. None of the above, and the projects have different roots.

Only the two sideways routes show a project obtaining work that its own upstream did not already hold. They are the strict test of decentralised exchange. The relayed route is the three-step movement described in the introduction, in which a change passes from its maker through an intermediary to a third project, with the intermediary on the third project's line.

4.4 Roles and dependence

Each project is given one role, tested in this order. An integrator has taken up work from at least 3 other owners, at least one of those take-ups arriving by a route other than down its own line. An originator is any other project whose own work has been taken up by another owner. A relay supplies nothing of its own, but another project received a change through it: it was the project on the taker's line that held a relayed change, or the source of a copy that was merged onward. A follower only receives, by take-up or by mirroring. An isolated project has no exchange with another owner in either direction. The classes describe what a project has done so far, and an integrator usually originates as well. The threshold of 3 owners is varied in Section 5.1.

To measure how far exchange rests on a few projects, we rank projects by the number of take-ups they are involved in as taker, as origin or as intermediary, remove the highest ranked, and recount. A take-up is removed with its taker, with its origin, and, where it passed through intermediaries, when all of them are removed. The same number of projects removed at random, averaged over a hundred draws with a fixed seed, gives the comparison [29]. What it misses: the recount is arithmetic on the record. It does not model what the remaining projects would have done in the absence of those removed.

4.5 Holdings and overlap

A project's holdings at a time T are the changes it held by then: those that had arrived, with a founding inheritance counted from the project's creation. At the end of each UTC day we compute, for every project in existence, the share of all changes in existence that it holds; for every pair of projects, the overlap of their holdings, the number of changes both hold divided by the number either holds; and for every change, the number of owners holding it. Within a family, each project is also compared with the family's most complete project, as the share of that project's holdings that it holds. Because new pairs join each day, we also follow fixed pairs: for the pairs in existence 24 hours before the snapshot, whether their overlap rose or fell by the snapshot. What it misses: mirrored history keeps its original times, so a project can appear to have held a change before it did; and the daily figures are reconstructed from the repositories that were still public when the population was first read.

4.6 Measures

Concentration is summarised by the Gini coefficient, the share held by the largest one and five, and the smallest number that account for half of the total, over projects, author identities or areas. Reciprocity is the share of project pairs with take-up in at least one direction that have it in both.

Time to first take-up cannot be read from completed take-ups alone, since the changes not yet taken up include many that are too young to have had the chance. We treat each change as observed from t0 until its first take-up or until the population was last read, and estimate the share taken up by a given age with the product-limit estimator of Kaplan and Meier [23]. The estimate is reported only up to the age that a tenth of the untaken changes have reached. As a check that makes no modelling assumption we also report, for changes old enough to have been observed for a fixed period, the plain share taken up within that period, and we report it separately for the changes of each UTC day once every change of that day has been observed for the period.

AI signature and take-up. A commit is treated as signed when its author field or message matches the signature a known agent leaves by default. Signed and unsigned changes come from different projects, and projects differ greatly in how much of their work is taken up, so a raw comparison is confounded. We restrict attention to projects that originated both kinds and treat each as a stratum. Two summaries are reported: the take-up rate of signed changes beside the rate unsigned changes would have if they were distributed over projects as the signed ones are, and the common odds ratio of Mantel and Haenszel [24]. Changes within a project are not independent, so the interval for the odds ratio is obtained by resampling whole projects with replacement 2,000 times and taking the central 95% of the results. The same estimator and interval are used for pull requests in Section 5.7, with receiving projects as strata.

Working rhythm. For each author identity the changes are ordered by author time. A sitting is a run of changes with no gap longer than 30 minutes. We compare signed and unsigned changes by the gap since the same author's previous change, by the length of sittings and by size, overall and within each author identity and each project that has at least 5 observations of both kinds. For identities with at least 30 changes we also take the longest run of hours of the day in which the identity made no change on any day of the window; this does not depend on the author's time zone. What it misses: author times are set by the author's machine and survive rebasing but not squashing; one identity can be several people or a person and their tools; and the window is short.

Kind of change. A change is classified from its stored subject line by a fixed rule. A subject whose first word is a form of revert, resolve or merge is a revert or a conflict resolution. Otherwise, if the subject opens with a label followed by a colon, a conventional label gives the kind (fix, feature, performance, tests, documentation, refactoring, or build, automation and tooling), a label containing the stem of test or of documentation gives that kind, and any other label marks a change named after a part of the code. A subject with no label is classified by a first word that is a form of fix, test, cover or document, and is otherwise unclassified. What it misses: subjects were stored with rare words removed. 64.0% of them lost at least one word and 7.9% begin with a removed word, where a label can no longer be read; and a change named after a part of the code can be of any kind.

Repeated work. A patch is repeated when the same fingerprint appears under a different author in a project of a different owner after it already existed elsewhere. A repeat is set aside if the repeating project, or a project on its upstream line, already held the earlier copy; what remains is a repeat in a project the earlier copy had not reached. What it misses: identical patches can come from a common source outside the record or from one person under two identities, and work repeated in different words is not detected.

Association with self-reported progress is measured by Spearman's rank correlation [39] between a project's latest recorded stage or reading and the take-ups it has received, with an interval from resampling projects, and again with the rank association of each with the project's age taken out.

Sensitivity. The headline share is recomputed under 10 alternative definitions, each changing one decision described above.

The population is a census of the repositories admitted, not a sample from a larger frame, so apart from the intervals just described we report counts and shares without significance tests.

4.7 Takedown notices

A notice is dated by the time the platform posted it. For a notice posted during the study we count the repositories created in equal periods before and after that time, for periods of 12 hours, 24 hours and 48 hours, and use the longest period that had fully elapsed on both sides when the population was last read. Counts are given for all recorded repositories, for the projects included under Section 3.2, for the repositories excluded, and for repositories that came from the community list and not from fork listings. To judge whether a difference at the notice is distinctive, the same comparison is made at every whole hour of the study, and we report at how many of those hours the ratio of after to before is at least as high as at the notice. For the same periods we compare the share of take-ups that were re-applied copies, the share that moved sideways, and the share of new projects that are not declared forks. A project is a declared fork if the platform names a parent for it or it was found through a fork listing. We look for projects created after a notice whose foundation is a project named in a notice or one that has ceased to be public, and for projects that are not declared forks and yet carry at least 100 inherited commits. Section 6.4 sets out what these comparisons can support.

AI signature. 14,161 changes (29.9%) carry an explicit AI agent signature in the commit itself (Claude 13,611, Codex 193, Cursor 177, Other agent 120, Copilot 60), across 154 projects. 26 projects sign every change they originate and 82 sign none.

6. Discussion

6.1 Does the population advance together?

In the weak sense, yes: work demonstrably moves. 19.1% of changes were merged or re-applied under another owner, 64.1% of the take-ups arriving within a day, and 2,491 changes are observed passing from their maker through an intermediary to a third project.

In the strong sense, the answer depends on the route, and at this snapshot exchange runs mainly along lines of foundation. 70.5% of take-ups are projects receiving what their own upstream line already held, and 17.0% are projects obtaining work from outside it. Exchange between families accounts for 3.8% of take-ups. 16.0% of exchanging pairs exchange in both directions. On these figures the population resembles a set of families, each with a few sources and many followers, more than it resembles a flat network of peers. The absence of a coordinating body has not produced an absence of structure: the fork relation supplies one by default, and most of the observed movement follows it.

Movement down a line is not a finding against decentralised progress. It is how an improvement made near the root of a family reaches the projects built on it, and relayed movement shows that work made in one branch of a family can reach another through their common upstream. But the two routes support different statements. Movement along lines is what many projects following a few would produce. Only sideways movement shows projects exchanging with each other.

Three further results qualify the picture. First, 98.9% of sideways take-ups are received by the 54 integrators, and removing five projects leaves 36.0% of all take-ups. Second, 49.0% of changes exist under one owner and 8.8% of cross-family pairs hold anything in common. Within families the projects are drifting apart: more pairs lost overlap over the last day than gained it. Third, the share of new changes taken up within six hours is higher on the last such day than on the first, without rising every day. A population can exchange more each day and still grow apart, if each project adds work of its own faster than it takes in the work of others.

6.2 What take-up does not measure

Take-up is evidence that an operation in another project brought a change in. It is the best signal Git offers that a change was of use to another team. It is not a measurement of benefit: establishing that a change made the software better needs before-and-after results on the same workload, which Git history does not contain, and the self-reported record of Section 5.8 does not supply it. It is also not a measurement of attention. A merge accepts a branch as a whole, so a change counted as taken up may never have been looked at by the project that now carries it. And absence of take-up is not rejection: a project may have solved the same problem in its own way, which leaves no matching patch.

6.3 Alternative explanations

Upkeep, not selection. A fork that has diverged from its source must merge to stay current, and each such merge is recorded as take-up of everything it brings. The route classification exists to isolate this, and the down-the-line share is its size.

One team, several repositories. Owners are accounts, not people. A person or team working under two accounts, or contributing to another team's repository from a fork of their own, produces cross-owner take-ups without any exchange between independent teams. The share of take-ups in which the author is also active in the taking project or under its owner (31.9%) measures the visible part of this. The count of identities with changes under more than one owner (761) also includes contributors whose work is credited to whichever project first held it.

A common source outside the population. If two projects each merge from a repository that is not in the study, the first to do so is recorded as the origin and the second as taking the change up sideways. Only 131 changes predate the first repository in the population, but outside repositories created during the window would not be detected in this way. The same holds for repeated patches.

Automation. Where agents or scheduled jobs perform merges, a take-up may reflect a standing instruction to stay current and not a decision about particular work. The record does not distinguish the two. Section 5.5 counts the identities that never sign and have no daily quiet spell of four hours.

Growth. 38.1% of the projects were created in the last 72 hours of the window. Where the population is growing, rates of exchange that rise with the number of projects need not mean that any project is exchanging more, and a fall in overlap between pairs can reflect new projects each adding work of their own.

6.4 What the notices analysis can and cannot show

Four features of the record limit it. First, forks were admitted by recency of push, which favours recently created repositories whatever happened in the window; the repositories from the community list are not selected in that way, and Table 18 gives them separately. Second, a repository disabled before the population was first read was never recorded. A notice processed against a whole network removes the forks that existed when it was processed, so the count before a notice is short by an unknown number and the comparison leans towards showing a rise. Third, the record is capped at 260 repositories, a small part of the networks the notices describe. Fourth, the window contains 1 of the 5 notices, too few for any inference about notices in general.

The analysis therefore supports descriptive statements only: how many recorded repositories and included projects were created on each side of a notice, how that compares with other hours of the same record, and how exchange stood on each side. It does not support a rate of repositories spawned by a notice, because what was created cannot be separated from what would have been created anyway, and it does not show that a notice had no effect, because the repositories it removed are missing from the count before it.

6.5 Implications

For studies of decentralised development, presence of the same change in two repositories supports very different statements depending on how it got there. The same ledger yields a travelling share anywhere between 1.2% and 29.6%. A figure of this kind should be reported with its definition, with the route breakdown, and with the number of acts behind the events. A pooled figure should be reported with its course over time where behaviour is changing. For the question of whether cheap production of changes moves the constraint to integration, the evidence here is indirect: 49.0% of changes are held only where they were made, 5.5% have moved between projects that are not on each other's line, and 227 patches were written again in a project the first copy had not reached.

7. Threats to validity

We follow the usual division into construct, internal and external validity and reliability [25].

7.1 Construct validity

Take-up stands in for usefulness and is not the same thing (Section 6.2). Matching on an identical patch undercounts borrowing that was re-typed, adapted or squashed, so all shares are lower bounds on reuse of ideas and, to a lesser degree, of code. Merged take-ups are counted per change although they are decided per merge. An owner is an account and not a team, and an author identity is an address and not a person. The AI signature records what a tool chose to write into a commit and is a floor on agent involvement: tools can be configured to leave none, and several projects state in their own documentation that they are built with agents while their commits carry no signature. The roles of Section 4.4 rest on thresholds, and the kind of a change on the wording of one line. The progress stage is a self-report read by a third party.

7.2 Internal validity

Origin is inferred from arrival order and can be wrong in the ways listed in Section 4.1, which moves credit between projects and can turn a take-up into a mirrored arrival or the reverse. The founding tree is inferred, and the route of a take-up depends on it; including declared fork parents in the upstream line makes the down-the-line classes more inclusive and the sideways classes conservative. Excluding a repository under Section 3.2 removes it from the upstream lines of the projects that descend from it. Commit times are set by the author's machine [17]. The number of commits whose author time is more than five minutes after their commit time is 3, the number dated after the snapshot is 0, and the number of take-ups dated before the change's recorded origin is 377. Take-ups delivered by one merge share one arrival time and are not independent observations, which affects every distribution of lags. The comparison of signed and unsigned changes controls for originating project and for nothing else. The product-limit estimate assumes that the chance of take-up at a given age does not depend on when a change was made, and Section 5.4 shows that it does. The history before the first read is reconstructed from repositories that survived to it, which bears on every daily figure and most of all on Section 5.9. The pull-request record is truncated for the busiest projects.

7.3 External validity

This is one case, on one hosting platform, observed for the first 8.6 days of its existence. Nothing here licenses a general claim about decentralised or AI-assisted development, or about the effect of takedown notices. The population is also selected. Forks are admitted by recency of push up to a cap per listed repository and a cap overall, which favours active forks, omits most inactive ones, and reaches forks of forks only when their parent is itself admitted; the inclusion bar then removes those that originated almost nothing. We expect shares with all projects in the denominator to be lower in the full fork network, and shares with changes in the denominator to be less affected, since inactive forks originate few changes.

7.4 Reliability

The analysis is deterministic given the ledger and the time of the run, including the resampling and the random removals, which use fixed seeds. The ledger is append-only and is retained, so the figures for any past snapshot can be regenerated, and one summary row per run is appended to it. Two things limit independent reproduction. Author and owner identities are stored as keyed hashes whose key is not published, so a third party can rerun the pipeline against the public sources but cannot reuse this ledger's grouping with data of their own. And repositories that have since been deleted or rewritten cannot be fetched again; for those the ledger is the only record.

8. Ethics and data handling

The study reads only public records: Git history, the platform's lists of pull requests, a public tracker, and the platform's published notices. Public availability does not by itself settle what may be done with such data [26], and the handling is designed to keep the study about projects and not about people. No person is named anywhere. Author identities are replaced at ingestion by keyed hashes, which are used to group commits into changes and are never published; only counts derived from them appear here. Commit subjects are passed through a filter that removes account names, personal names, addresses, links and rare words before they are stored. Projects are shown by repository name without the owning account, and owners appear only as numbered groups. Directory names are stored without file names and are not shown. This site carries no links to the projects, no builds and no downloads, and the ledger holds no source code.

For takedown notices the ledger holds where each notice is published, when it was posted, and counts. The repositories a notice names are compared with the record by keyed hash and are not stored, and nothing here identifies a named repository, its owner or the party that filed the notice. The study takes no position on any notice.

One choice favours the completeness of the record over the later wishes of an owner: when a repository is removed from public view, the study keeps the metadata it had already recorded. What is kept is a commit graph with fingerprints and scrubbed subject lines, not content, and dropping removed projects would bias every measure of persistence. The paper describes behaviour in aggregate and makes no assessment of any project, team or tool.

9. Future work

Five extensions would address the main limits. First, matching changes by similarity and not identity would show how much borrowing and how much repeated work the patch fingerprint misses. Second, keeping each project's branch heads from every reading, and not only the latest, would show whether a change once taken up is later dropped; the persistence of take-up cannot be measured without it. Third, a longer window would allow removal to be studied: which projects stop, which are removed, whether those removed had been named in a notice, and whether their work lives on in others. At this snapshot the number of projects removed stands at 20. Fourth, a record of repositories taken from the platform's full fork listings at each reading, and not from the most recently pushed, would remove the selection that limits Section 5.9. Fifth, relating take-up to an external measure of whether the software improved would turn a study of movement into a study of progress. A comparison population working without AI agents, if one of similar density could be found, would be needed before anything here could be attributed to their use.

10. Conclusion

In a population of 236 projects with no coordinating body, 19.1% of 47,336 changes were taken up by a project of another owner, half of them within 8.0 hours. Work does move between independent projects, and 2,491 changes are seen moving onward through an intermediary. 70.5% of that movement follows the lines along which projects were founded on one another, and 5.5% of changes have moved between projects that are not on each other's line. Half of it is supplied by 6 projects, and without the five most involved projects 36.0% of it remains. 49.0% of changes are held under one owner, and the overlap between projects of one family has fallen to 19.7%. The share of new changes taken up within six hours is higher on the last such day than on the first, without rising every day. Around the takedown notice that could be assessed, 82 recorded repositories were created in the 48 hours before and 171 in the 48 hours after, and the record does not support a figure for repositories spawned by a notice. As a set of families within which work passes along lines of foundation, the evidence is 70.5% of take-ups. As a network of peers that take each other's work, it is 17.0%. The measurements will be repeated as the population ages, and the definitions under which each figure holds are stated so that they can be contested.
